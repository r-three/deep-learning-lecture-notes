Lecture 3: Under/overfitting, Regularization, Numerical Stability, Autodiff

Lecture 3: Under/overfitting, Regularization, Numerical Stability, Autodiff

Recap. In Lecture 2, we derived backpropagation: a base case at the output layer, a backward recursion through the weight transpose, and an outer product to recover weight gradients. In this lecture, we ask what backprop costs, work through a full example that reveals why gradients can vanish or explode, use that insight to derive a principled way to initialize parameters, see how automatic differentiation turns backprop into a mechanical procedure a computer can run for us, and finally, turn to overfitting: why models memorize training data, and the two regularizers (i.e., weight decay and dropout) used to stop them.

Backpropagation, Continued

Recap: The Three Steps

Recall from Backpropagation the three ingredients of backpropagation, for an L-layer model with pre-activations a^m, activations h^m = \sigma^m(a^m), and loss \mathcal{L}:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^L} \odot \frac{dh^L}{da^L} \qquad\text{(base case)} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m} = \left({W^{m+1}}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{m+1}}\right) \odot \frac{dh^m}{da^m} \qquad\text{(recursion, } m = L-1, \ldots, 1\text{)} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^m} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m} \cdot (h^{m-1})^\top \qquad\text{(weight gradient)}

The Cost of Backpropagation

Suppose the model is a stack of matrix multiplies and element-wise nonlinearities. Which is more expensive: a matrix multiply, or an element-wise nonlinearity? The matrix multiply. It does one multiply-add per entry of the weight matrix, and a weight matrix has far more entries than the layer has units. So the cost of a layer is dominated by its matrix multiply, and the forward pass costs roughly one multiply per parameter, summed over all layers: O(|\theta|), where |\theta| is the total number of parameters.

Now, look at the backward recursion above. It also does a matrix–vector product with W^{m+1} at every layer. The same asymptotic cost as the forward pass and the weight-gradient step is an outer product that produces an object as large as W^m itself, again, with the same cost. So:

Pass What it computes Cost
Forward a^m, h^m for all m \sim |\theta|
Backward: recursion \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial a^m for all m \sim |\theta|
Backward: weight grads \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial W^m for all m \sim |\theta|
Note

Rule of thumb. One training step (forward + backward) costs about three forward passes: \sim 3|\theta|. This is why people often estimate training compute directly from parameter count.

Worked Example: A Sigmoid Network

To see why initialization matters, let’s push the recursion through a concrete network: sigmoid activations at every layer, including the output (h^m = \operatorname{sigmoid}(a^m) for all m, including m=L), trained with the binary cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}(y, h^L) = (y-1)\log(1-h^L) - y\log(h^L)

Base case. Applying the chain rule, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^L}\frac{dh^L}{da^L}, where the first factor is the cross-entropy derivative and the second is the sigmoid derivative:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^L} = \underbrace{\frac{h^L - y}{h^L(1-h^L)}}_{\text{cross-entropy}} \cdot \underbrace{h^L(1-h^L)}_{\text{sigmoid}'} \;=\; h^L - y

The h^L(1-h^L) terms cancel exactly, leaving the same remarkably clean result we saw for softmax in Cross-Entropy Loss: the gradient is simply the prediction error.

One step of the recursion. Substituting into the backward recursion:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-1}} = \left({W^L}^\top (h^L - y)\right) \odot h^{L-1}(1-h^{L-1})

and the corresponding weight gradient is \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{L-1}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-1}} \cdot (h^{L-2})^\top. Every further step of the recursion just multiplies in one more weight matrix transpose and one more sigmoid-derivative factor. After many layers, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m} is a product of many (W^{k})^\top terms and many sigmoid-derivative terms, chained together.

Vanishing and Exploding Gradients

Two repeated multiplications drive the recursion, and each can misbehave:

The nonlinearity term. The sigmoid’s derivative is at most \tfrac14 (at x=0) and is otherwise smaller. Multiplying numbers between 0 and \tfrac14 together, over and over, drives the product toward 0 exponentially fast in the number of layers. Since this factor appears once per layer in the recursion, the gradient reaching the early layers of a deep sigmoid network is scaled by roughly (\tfrac14)^L and it vanishes. And if \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^m} \approx 0, the gradient-descent update W^m \leftarrow W^m - \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^m} barely changes W^m at all. The early layers simply stop learning.

Warning

This is why sigmoid is rarely used as a hidden-layer activation. ReLU’s derivative is exactly 0 or exactly 1. Multiplying by 1 repeatedly neither shrinks nor grows the gradient. This is one of the reasons ReLU made training much deeper networks practical.

The weight-matrix term. The recursion also repeatedly multiplies by (W^{k})^\top. What happens when you multiply a matrix by itself over and over depends on its eigenvalues: values consistently above 1 tend to blow the product up (exploding gradients); values consistently below 1 tend to shrink it to zero (vanishing again). Unlike the nonlinearity term, this direction of failure is entirely in our hands. It’s set by how we choose the weight matrices before training even starts.

Parameter Initialization

Gradient descent only ever nudges parameters by a small step in the direction of the negative gradient — the initial values are the one place we have direct control, before training dynamics take over. So we should choose that starting distribution carefully.

Two bad ideas, first. Why not initialize every weight to 0? Then every layer’s output stops depending on the input entirely (ignoring the bias), the model can’t learn any input–output mapping, and every gradient is 0 too — nothing updates. Why not make every weight the same constant, say 1? Then every row of W computes the same dot product with the input, so every hidden unit learns an identical, totally redundant function — we waste nearly all of the layer’s capacity. We need random, but carefully-scaled, initial values.

Deriving Xavier / Glorot Initialization

Consider one linear layer in isolation, \bm{o} = W\bm{x}, with \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{in}} and \bm{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{out}}. Assume no nonlinearity, x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), and W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) — the only knob we control is \sigma.

Since o_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{in}} W_{ij}\, x_j is a sum of zero-mean terms, \mathbb{E}[o_i] = 0. For the variance, using \mathrm{Var}[o_i] = \mathbb{E}[o_i^2] - \mathbb{E}[o_i]^2 = \mathbb{E}[o_i^2] and independence of W and x:

\mathrm{Var}[o_i] = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{in}} \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2]\,\mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{in}} \sigma^2 \cdot 1 = n_{in}\,\sigma^2

Forward pass. If we want activations to keep the same scale layer after layer (variance \approx 1, matching the input), we need n_{in}\sigma^2 = 1.

Backward pass. The same argument applied to the backward recursion (which multiplies by W^\top instead of W) requires n_{out}\sigma^2 = 1 to keep gradient variance stable.

We can’t satisfy both exactly unless n_{in} = n_{out}, so we average the two constraints as a compromise: \frac{n_{in}\sigma^2 + n_{out}\sigma^2}{2} = 1, giving:

\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{in} + n_{out}}}

This is Xavier (or Glorot) initialization (Glorot and Bengio 2010) — sample every weight from \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) with this \sigma. Because the activation variance stays \approx 1 at every layer, neither the forward activations nor the backward gradients can systematically explode or vanish.

Note

He initialization. Xavier’s derivation assumes no nonlinearity. If you instead assume a ReLU activation (as Homework 3 does), the same style of derivation gives a slightly different scale, called He initialization (He et al. 2015). Most deep learning frameworks default to one of these two schemes.

Automatic Differentiation

So far, every gradient in this course was derived by hand: we wrote down the chain rule for a specific loss and a specific set of layers. Automatic differentiation (autodiff, or autograd) turns this into a mechanical procedure that a computer performs for any composition of functions you define — it is what powers every modern deep learning framework (PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX, …) under the hood.

The trick is to decompose computation into elementary operations (add, multiply, subtract, raise-to-a-power, ReLU, …) simple enough that we can write down each one’s derivative once, in general, and never think about it again.

A Worked Example

Take a tiny scalar network: a hidden unit h = \operatorname{ReLU}(w_h x + b_h), an output o = w_o h + b_o, and squared error loss \mathcal{L} = (y - o)^2. To automatically differentiate, break every line into one elementary operation at a time:

m_h = w_h \cdot x \qquad a_h = m_h + b_h \qquad h = \operatorname{ReLU}(a_h) m_o = w_o \cdot h \qquad o = m_o + b_o \qquad e = y - o \qquad \mathcal{L} = e^2

Each line is one op: multiply, add, ReLU, multiply, add, subtract, power. This is about as far as we can decompose things — and there are only a few dozen such ops in total, shared and reused everywhere.

The diagram above is the computational graph: a directed acyclic graph where each circle is either an operation node (computed from its inputs) or a leaf node (an input or a parameter — the things we might want a derivative with respect to). To compute \frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dw_h}, we find the path in the graph from \mathcal{L} down to w_h, and multiply the local derivative at every operation node along the way:

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dw_h} = \frac{d\mathcal{L}}{de}\cdot\frac{de}{do}\cdot\frac{do}{dm_o}\cdot\frac{dm_o}{dh}\cdot\frac{dh}{da_h}\cdot\frac{da_h}{dm_h}\cdot\frac{dm_h}{dw_h}

Every factor here is the derivative of a single, simple operation — exactly the kind of thing we can hard-code once per op and never re-derive. (If a node has more than one path to \mathcal{L}, sum the contributions of each path — this is exactly the “multiple intermediate variables” case of the chain rule from Backpropagation.)

Automatic Differentiation

Define, once, for a small library of elementary ops (add, multiply, subtract, power, ReLU, …):

  • forward: how to compute the op’s output from its inputs.
  • backward: the derivative of the op’s output with respect to each input.

Given a user’s model (a composition of these ops):

  1. Forward pass — evaluate every op in order, and record which ops were applied to which inputs. This record is the computational graph.
  2. Backward pass — to get d\mathcal{L}/dw for any leaf w, traverse the graph from \mathcal{L} to w, multiplying local derivatives along every path (summing over paths if there is more than one).

This is exactly what Homework 3 asks you to build: an Operation class (with add, multiply, … subclasses) that knows its own forward and backward rules, a graph that records which operations were applied, and a backward() that walks the graph to produce every gradient automatically — no more hand-deriving a custom backward pass per architecture.

Overfitting and Regularization

Recall the Universal Approximation Theorem (Universal Approximation Theorem): a sufficiently wide network can fit any function, including simply memorizing a finite training set. So overfitting is a real risk, and it isn’t specific to neural networks at all; the same ideas apply to any sufficiently flexible model.

The classic symptom (left) is a gap that opens up between training and validation loss: training loss keeps falling while validation loss bottoms out and starts rising. The classic picture of why (right): given noisy samples of some true function, a good fit stays close to the true curve everywhere, while an overfit model contorts itself to pass exactly through every training point — at the cost of wild, high-error excursions everywhere else.

How Can a Linear Model Overfit?

It’s tempting to think a model as simple as \hat{y} = wx can’t overfit. But consider: each feature x_i is sampled from x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(y_i, \sigma) where y_i \in \{-1, +1\} is the label — i.e., every feature is a noisy version of the label, so its distribution for the two classes overlaps heavily. With enough features and a small enough training set, it’s entirely possible that pure chance makes one particular feature perfectly linearly separable on the training data alone. The model can then achieve zero training loss by placing all of its weight on that one lucky feature and ignoring every other — a classic case of over-reliance on a feature that has no reason to generalize.

The same failure mode shows up in deep networks: with many learned features and a limited training set, some feature may become accidentally separable, and the model over-invests in it. Both regularizers below attack exactly this over-reliance.

L2 Regularization (Weight Decay)

To discourage any single weight from growing too large, add the squared L2 norm of the weights to the loss, scaled by a hyperparameter \lambda:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \mathcal{L} + \lambda \lVert W \rVert_2^2

The gradient descent update on this regularized loss is

W \leftarrow W - \eta \nabla_W\!\left(\mathcal{L} + \lambda \lVert W \rVert_2^2\right) = (1 - 2\eta\lambda)\, W - \eta \nabla_W \mathcal{L}

Since \eta and \lambda are both small, (1-2\eta\lambda) is a number just under 1 — every update multiplicatively shrinks W a little, in addition to the usual gradient step. This is why L2 regularization is also called weight decay.

Dropout

The second common regularizer, dropout (Srivastava et al. 2014) (invented at the University of Toronto), attacks over-reliance more directly: during training, each feature is independently zeroed out with probability p (a hyperparameter), and otherwise rescaled by \frac{1}{1-p}:

x_i' = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{with probability } p \\[4pt] \dfrac{x_i}{1-p} & \text{with probability } 1-p \end{cases}

The rescaling is chosen precisely so that \mathbb{E}[x_i'] = x_i — dropout doesn’t change what the network computes on average, but because any given feature might vanish on any given training step, the model can never afford to rely on just one. Weight decay and dropout are, in practice, the two regularizers you will reach for most often in deep learning.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Lecture 3

  • Backpropagation costs about 3× a forward pass: \sim|\theta| for the forward pass, and \sim2|\theta| more for the backward pass (a matrix–vector product plus an outer product per layer).
  • A sigmoid network’s backward recursion repeatedly multiplies by (a) the sigmoid derivative (max \tfrac14) and (b) the weight matrix transpose — the first reliably vanishes, the second can vanish or explode depending on its eigenvalues.
  • Xavier/Glorot initialization (\sigma = \sqrt{2/(n_{in}+n_{out})}) keeps activation and gradient variance \approx 1 at every layer, avoiding vanishing/exploding gradients from the start of training. He initialization is the ReLU-specific variant.
  • Automatic differentiation decomposes any model into elementary operations with known local derivatives, records them in a computational graph during the forward pass, and computes every gradient by traversing that graph backward — no hand-derived backward pass required.
  • Overfitting happens when a model over-relies on a feature that is accidentally informative on the training set alone. Weight decay (+\lambda\lVert W\rVert_2^2, i.e. shrink weights every step) and dropout (randomly zero features during training) both discourage this over-reliance.
